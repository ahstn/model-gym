Five Exa searches covered authentic quality, MLLM assessment, pairwise judgements, generated-image quality, and AI-origin detection. Each requested eight results: 40 requested result slots, 40 returned URL entries, and 40 unique exact URLs. Some URLs describe the same work. Search results are candidate sources, not 40 independently verified studies. Selected author pages, papers, repositories and licence files were then read. Evidence is in `evidence/catalog-exa.json`, `catalog-exa-followup.json`, `catalog-awesome-pin.json`, `catalog-awesome-readme.md`, `catalog-links.json` and `catalog-terms.json`.

The best immediate expansion is human-rated technical quality with defect labels, followed by generated-image quality and a separate AI-origin benchmark. Preserve the old test set. Do not use future holdout images or labels to write the rubric.

## Methods and prompt strategies

[Q-Align](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.17090) uses five text-defined rating levels rather than direct decimal scores. Its released scorer converts probabilities for excellent, good, fair, poor and bad into a weighted average. This supports a Decider trial with named levels and explicit anchors. It does not prove that requesting five probabilities from an API has the same effect as fine-tuning or reading token logits. The Q-Align repository has S-Lab non-commercial terms.

[Q-Instruct](https://q-future.github.io/Q-Instruct/) supplies low-level instruction data and demonstrations. Use its distinction between observing defects and judging quality to design the response format. It is training material, not an independent human MOS target for every instruction. The repository permits researchers to use the dataset and weights; commercial use requires advance permission under its published terms.

[Co-Instruct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16641) supports pairwise and multi-image quality comparison with explanations. Its 562,000 training items include merged descriptions and GPT-4V teacher responses. Thus, the training data must not be described as entirely direct human targets. Test pairwise decisions with image order reversed and report agreement. Use human MOS or genuine human comparisons as the final target. [DepictQA](https://github.com/XPixelGroup/DepictQA) also separates score-free descriptions, comparisons and confidence; its source code is Apache-2.0, while model and dataset dependencies need separate checks.

[PACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22942), a September 2026 preprint, describes holistic bias: a model can give a high dimension score because the whole image appears pleasing. It replaces an implicit judgement with visible probes and calibrates a new dimension using four human-rated examples. This is a close research match to Decider's suspected content bias. Its reported gains are author results, not local proof. A lightweight trial can test the probe-first principle without reproducing its agent system.

[SDG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06113), a June 2026 preprint, represents a defect by location, type, reason and importance. It describes 30,096 generated images across four generators and a dedicated defect evaluation protocol. The useful response idea is a short, grounded defect record before the quality level. This differs from provenance evidence: malformed anatomy can reduce quality but cannot, by itself, establish AI origin. Dataset terms remain to be checked.

[PromptIQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04993) uses image-score pairs as prompts in a trained quality model. It supports testing development-only calibration examples. It is not evidence that a text-only rubric can replace visual anchors.

## Proposed Decider trial design

These are research-derived hypotheses for the authorised prompt trials. Their effect requires measurement.

1. Separate technical quality, aesthetics, naturalness, prompt alignment and AI-origin probability. Keep the current public output contract until the task owner approves any schema change.
2. Try a five-level technical rubric: bad means severe widespread degradation; poor means strong defects; fair means visible moderate defects; good means minor defects; excellent means negligible visible defects at the delivered view. A recognisable subject must not raise technical quality on its own. Calibrate numerical anchors from development human scores rather than inventing decimal precision.
3. Require observations for blur, noise, compression or ringing, exposure and clipping, colour or contrast, and local structural defects. Record region and severity only where visible. Do not count absent detail at a resized view as proof of a defect in the source.
4. Compare a short rubric, a defect-first rubric, a named-level rubric and development visual anchors. Add pairwise ranking as a separate trial if the input path supports two images. Keep the prompt length and inference cost in the results.
5. Fit score calibration only on development predictions and human MOS. Report raw and calibrated MAE, rank correlation, and low-quality recall at a fixed threshold. Report false positives, subgroup results and uncertainty. A monotonic calibration can improve scale agreement while leaving rank correlation unchanged.
6. Use independent source groups: controlled KADID defects, authentic KonIQ or SPAQ, and generated AGIQA. Hold out source images and their variants together. Use one complete external dataset as a final transfer test where access permits. Do not merge labels merely because their scores use similar numbers.
7. Optimise prompts against human quality targets and selected defect targets. Weight or stratify low-quality development examples so that a strong average score cannot conceal poor detection of severe defects. Longer GEPA search can overfit; compare fixed candidates across seeds and select from development results only.
8. For origin detection, use balanced real/generated sources and an unseen-generator holdout. Match resize and JPEG processing between classes. Report AUROC, precision-recall, false-positive rate on real images, and calibration. [Fake or JPEG?](https://www.unbiased-genimage.org/) shows that size and compression bias in GenImage can distort detector conclusions. Treat the processed-view policy as part of the benchmark.

The prior 80-image KADID result provided by the task owner has MAE 0.970, rank correlation 0.781 and low-quality recall 6/40. That combination supports investigating score calibration and threshold failures; it does not establish that more optimisation alone will fix perceptual errors. The proposed authentic-image source and defect diagnostics can distinguish these causes. No paid model call, new holdout inspection, dataset image download or old-test change was made for this catalogue.

### Optional AGIQA panel decision

This pass excludes the proposed 80-image numeric AGIQA external panel. [The primary paper, section III-C](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.04717v2), gives a raw slider range of 0 to 5, followed by session normalisation, conversion to z-scores, and a rescaling operation called Res(z). That section does not define numeric rescaling anchors. Its perception score also combines technical defects, AI artefacts, apparent falseness and aesthetics. These facts prevent a defensible direct conversion into technical-quality gold labels on the current 1 to 5 scale. The MIT database licence is confirmed; the scoring construct and scale are the limiting factors. No unused images were acquired. A future panel could measure rank correlation against the native perception MOS, with the mixed construct stated in its objective.
